

Frozen
------

# Inciting moment (Point of no return)

The glove comes off, ~25% — the world changes irreversibly.

At the coronation party when Anna pulls Elsa's glove off during their argument — and Elsa, cornered, blasts a wall of ice spikes across the ballroom in front of everyone. Thirteen years of concealment gone in a second.

# Textbook Act 1 --> Act 2 mechanics kicking in immediately:

The door slam ends Act 1 emphatically — Elsa's choice is made, no going back.

The very next scene launches the quest:
  - new goal (find Elsa), 
  - new setting (the mountain), 
  - key Act 2 ally introduced (Kristoff, plus Sven).
  - Tonal reset — from soaring power ballad to broad comedy in one cut. 

Screenwriters often follow a big act break with a lighter beat to let the audience breathe.


Mandabi
-------

1. There's a textbook inciting incident: the money order from Dieng's nephew in Paris arrives, disrupting his ordinary world completely. Word spreads, creditors and relatives descend, and the quest begins — cash the order.

But then the Hollywood pattern breaks down:

- No "door locks" choice. Dieng never gets a "I changed my mind" moment. He doesn't choose the quest; it's imposed on him, and he remains reactive throughout.

- The middle isn't escalation, it's repetition. Instead of a rising Act 2 driven by the hero's decisions, Sembène gives you a loop — office, queue, official, rejection, again. The repetition is the point: the bureaucracy is a machine that grinds, not a mountain to climb.

- The "all is lost" beat exists but doesn't turn. Mbaye, the westernised businessman, swindles Dieng out of the money order entirely. In Hollywood that's the end of Act 2, and Act 3 would be Dieng's fight back. Instead the film simply... stops rising.

Sembène refuses the empowered-individual arc because his subject is a system in which individual agency is precisely what's denied. The form matches the argument.
